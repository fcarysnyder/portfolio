Clarity is the Deliverable

Product hands the team more requirements than it can build and engineering can build almost any of them. Design is the work of deciding which part is signal.

July 21, 2026·9 min read

Every product development team has the same basic dynamic. Product owns the requirements, which arrive as volume: a Jira backlog hundreds of tickets deep, a channel of customer feedback, a running list of everything that could be built. Engineering sits on the other side, ready to build whatever it gets pointed at. So the team has a list of many things they could do and a crew that can build any of them.

Someone has to bring that direction. It means taking everything - business pressure, feasibility, conversations with the people who use the product - and coming back with a stance. Something like "here's the thing, for these users, and here's why it's the one we do now," with enough detail that engineering can build it and product can measure it.

That work is design. Not the noun; the verb. Someone trained as a designer is usually best equipped to do it, but nothing about it is role-bound.

What does design actually do then?

Design collapses an overwhelming field of possibilities into direction and momentum. Every decision is a small version of the same move: pick one, and put the rest to the side. The artifact of design records those decisions and makes them visible, but the value sits in the reduction itself.

Visual design is important, critical even. But it falls flat if the underlying decisions are poorly conceived. A tool has never made something well designed. Looking beautiful is downstream of deciding what the thing is, and crucially, what it isn't.

How to deliver clarity

The best way to bring clarity is to show up with a strong opinion, loosely held, about what to do next and let people react to it.

Asking what it would take to build is a different move, and that question makes a proposal better rather than safer. But a map drawn before any contact with reality is a defense.
