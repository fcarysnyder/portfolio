> **DRAFT / STUB.** Captured 2026-08-04 from a real SIG UX conversation. Not for publication. The companion piece [Clarity is the Deliverable](/writing/clarity-is-the-deliverable) assumes reduction is always the job; this piece is the exception it doesn't cover — the phase where you deliberately hold reduction off. Likely the next entry after [The Bad Ideas Look Shippable](/writing/the-bad-ideas-look-shippable) in the prototyping series.

## The core idea

The normal product development process is: ideas → form a squad → design, build, ship → harden into the design system and maintain it long-term. Sequential. Reduction happens early, before the build.

That's not what's happening right now. Product, engineering, and design are all hacking prototypes together — Claude, v0, whatever — running a wall of generation at once, because we're exploring new ventures and hunting for what has product-market fit. In that mode, high velocity is correct, UX is all over the place on purpose, and design quality drops for a while. That is not a failure. It's the right posture for the phase.

Once something gets sticky — once customers validate it — *that's* when it goes through the design process and gets hardened into something we maintain. ("Harden" is probably the wrong verb. Find a better one.)

So the model has two phases:

1. **Explore at velocity.** Generate broadly, chase several problems at once, accept messy UX, get things in front of customers fast. Reduction is premature here.
2. **Harden with design.** Something stuck. Now collapse it into a coherent direction, put it in the design system, maintain it long-term.

The mistake is applying phase-2 standards to phase-1 work, or letting phase-1 sprawl never get hardened. Both break the team.

## The actionable (shared understanding)

The thing I actually asked the team for, and what made it land: **when you make a prototype, say what you're testing.**

- If you were testing information architecture, we ignore the five interactions you built down some tree — that was you exploring, having a good time, fine. Not for us to react to.
- If you were testing the interactions, then we pay attention to them.

Without the label, receivers get lost: "why is there a whole interaction model here?" With it, everyone knows what to engage with and what to let go. (This principle also lives, compressed, in the Clarity piece. Here it gets room and a concrete example.)

## Raw material — the SIG UX story

First UX special interest group meeting. Opened by acknowledging design is *okay* with the current mess: give us a pile of prototypes, UX all over the place, we're just getting ideas out and getting customer validation, and design is doing the same. Design quality drops during this window, and that's the deal. Then, when something's sticky, we harden it through the design process.

Manager's read: good approach for a first SIG — get everyone (design, eng, product) on the same page about velocity, validation, then hardening. He pushed on "harden" as the verb.

Why it matters for the writing: this is a live, real-world proof that the framing works. Took a stance into a room, people reacted, it moved. Use it.

## Open questions

- Better verb than "harden." (Solidify? Commit? Ratify? Productionize?)
- Where's the boundary between the phases? What signal says "this is sticky, start hardening"?
- Does design quality *have* to drop, or is that just the current tooling reality? Is there a version where phase-1 stays cheap without getting ugly?
- Risk to preempt: this reads as license to ship slop. It isn't. The discipline is in the phase transition, not the permission to be messy.
