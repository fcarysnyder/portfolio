Every product development team has the same basic dynamic. Product owns the requirements, which arrive as volume: a Jira backlog hundreds of tickets deep, a channel of customer feedback, a running list of everything that could be built. Engineering sits on the other side, ready to build whatever it gets pointed at. Most engineers I've worked with will tell you anything is possible with an infinite timeline, and that any given feature has many ways to build it. So the team has a list of many things they *could* do and a crew that can build any of them. All of it stays noise until somebody says which part is signal: build this, for these users, now.

Someone has to bring that direction. It means taking everything - business pressure, feasibility, conversations with the people who use the product - and coming back with a stance. Something like "here's the thing, for these users, and here's why it's the one we do now," with enough detail that engineering can build it and product can measure it.

That work is design. Not the noun; the verb. Someone trained as a designer is usually best equipped to do it, but nothing about it is role-bound. In plenty of orgs I've worked in, the customer research lives with product managers and design picks it up from there. The PM who sits with three customers and comes back with "this is the one that matters, and here's what it has to do" is doing design work without the title.

Design is a forcing function. "How about this" gets you a "yes", a "no", or a "not like that", and all three put you further along than the conversation you were having before. An engineer can tell you "that'll take two quarters, not two sprints". Support can tell you customers hated that in the old product. Product can say that's nice, but it doesn't move the needle for the business. None of that comes back when the idea is still vague.

One caveat on scope. I'm describing product development: features, flows, the things a person ends up using. Data migrations, backend services with hard contracts, anything where being wrong is expensive to undo, all of that has a different shape, and plenty of it genuinely does need the full map before anyone starts. What follows applies where the options are many, the right one isn't obvious, and you can still afford to change your mind.

## What does design actually do then?

Design collapses an overwhelming field of possibilities into direction and momentum. Every decision is a small version of the same move: pick one, and put the rest to the side. The artifact of design - often a Figma design, but can include sketches, writing, code and other mediums - records those decisions and makes them visible, but the value sits in the reduction itself.

The problem is when design is viewed only as a visual discipline, particularly in UI, UX, and Product Design. People hear "design" and picture a UI mockup. They think of the interior designer they hired to redo their room. Or the graphic design of their favorite movie poster. A canva template. Design is difficult because the word itself has been diluted in society to ultimately mean the way something looks. And because anyone can have an opinion about how something looks, ultimately that's what gets talked about most.

Visual design is important, critical even. But it falls flat if the underlying decisions are poorly conceived. A tool has never made something well designed. ShadCN, a pristine design system, an interface an LLM produced in a minute: each one raises the floor on how a product looks, and not one of them decides what the product should be. Looking beautiful is downstream of deciding what the thing is, and crucially, what it isn't. These decisions stack and add up to *become* the design. The pixels of a Figma design are just the record of it.

## LLMs make this harder and more important at the same time

LLMs expand the possibility space faster than any team can process it. More options, more directions, more outputs that all look plausible enough to consider. The noise gets louder than signal. The bad ideas look shinier. The asymmetry between "things that can be generated" and "things a team can actually evaluate and commit to" widens every quarter.

The LLMs are good, they're getting better, and we can't bury our heads in the sand and pretend they aren't here to stay. Product development roles are blurring at the same time: engineers reach for design tools, designers reach for code, product reaches into both. The work no longer fits cleanly inside any one role's borders. The cost of producing options has collapsed. The cost of choosing between them hasn't. If anything, choosing has gotten harder, because every option now arrives wearing a coat of polished detail that used to take weeks to apply.

Order matters here more than volume. Product and engineering generate because they're chasing several problems at once, so the output spreads outward. The move is to decide what you're solving first, then point the tools at that one thing and generate twenty versions of it. Generation after a decision compounds. Generation in place of a decision just builds a bigger pile.

The same order applies to what arrives from other people. When a teammate hands you fifty generated tickets or an eight-page spec, the job isn't to read it politely and add to it, it's to come back with the two things that matter and say the rest can wait. Skip that reduction and the team turns into a generation engine with nobody steering: more output every week, and less agreement about what any of it is for.

It cuts both ways. When you're the one generating, say what you're testing. A prototype handed over as "does this information architecture hold?" tells the room to ignore the twelve interactions you didn't mean seriously, and saves everyone a day spent reacting to a question you weren't asking. Fidelity with no label gets read as a proposal, every time.

The judgment problem didn't go away, it got bigger. What counts as good design - taste - didn't change either; only what counts as fast did. Reducing noise to a stance the team can stress-test runs at the speed of clear thinking, which is the same speed it has always run at.

The tools keep getting better at making things exist. Whether a thing should exist is a different question, and nothing about better tooling makes it easier to answer.

## How to deliver clarity

The best way to bring clarity is to show up with a strong opinion, loosely held, about what to do next and let people react to it.

This is the move that separates people who unblock teams from people who get stuck defending themselves. A good proposal doesn't need a defense. It needs an audience. When you walk into a room with something concrete, a prototype or a spec or a decision written down, you change the conversation from abstract to specific, from spinning to building. People stop debating in the abstract because there's now a thing in front of them, and the thing is more interesting than the debate.

Not everyone meets a half-formed direction comfortably. Some people will want to slow it down: map every state, resolve every edge case, get all of it agreed before a line of code exists. The instinct makes sense, because an undecided thing feels like exposure. Asking what it would take to build is a different move, and [that question makes a proposal better rather than safer](/writing/the-bad-ideas-look-shippable). But a map drawn before any contact with reality is a defense. It's a castle with a moat dug around it, built so that nobody can get close enough to ask a hard question.

The tell is who the artifact serves. A proposal is built to be reacted to, which is why it survives an engineer saying two quarters or support saying customers hated that. A fortification is built to survive review. Both take real work, and only one of them moves the team.

None of this depends on being right. A proposal gets things wrong regularly, and anyone good at this expects that and goes again. A clear wrong answer is more useful than a fuzzy right one, because a wrong answer can be argued with. A fuzzy one just absorbs another meeting.

Clarity is the deliverable. The artifact, whatever the medium, is only the mechanism, and what you actually hand the team is a change in what the room is doing. Before, ten people were generating. After, ten people are reacting to one thing. The noise didn't go down because the team got smarter overnight. It went down because somebody made a move.

## The whole job

Design, the verb, brings clarity to noise. That's it. Taking an overwhelming field of possibilities and collapsing it into a single coherent direction that lets everyone stop debating and start building.

Everything else - the components, the specs, the prototypes, the systems - is in service of that. That's what design is for, and it's why anyone in the room can pick it up: the work belongs to whoever sees the noise and decides to give it shape. Titles will keep moving around. The work stays where it is.

AI doesn't change the core of our job. As fidelity and noise rise, design's job is to adapt to the input, smooth the handoff to engineering, and make sure the result is usable and solves user problems. The tools change. The job doesn't.
